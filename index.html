Is Earth rare?

762 exoplanets confirmed by NASA's TESS mission. Only one dot is Earth's twin. Or is it even there at all?

TESS — the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite — has been scanning the sky since 2018, finding planets that briefly dim their host star as they pass in front of it. But the telescope can only see what its design allows. This visualization explores what TESS finds, what it misses, and where Earth would sit if we could see everything.

The full catalog

762 exoplanets confirmed by NASA's TESS mission — each dot representing a real world orbiting a real star. The axes show temperature (how hot the planet's surface gets) and size (relative to Earth).

The dashed line at 1.8 R⊕ is the Fulton radius gap — a real scarcity of planets at exactly that size, likely caused by stellar radiation stripping their atmospheres.

Rocky worlds like Earth

Only 54 are rocky like Earth — just 7% of the catalog. Everything else is a gas giant, ice giant, or something in between.

"Rocky" or terrestrial planets, are planets containing solid surfaces and are the most habitable planet type. We estimate them as those smaller than 1.25 Earth radii (R⊕). Above that threshold, planets likely retain thick hydrogen atmospheres.

The mystery class

259 are Sub-Neptunes — the single most common type, with radii between 2 and 4 R⊕. Our Solar System has nothing like them. They sit between Earth and Neptune in size but their composition remains poorly understood.

These planets above the Fulton radius gap are rarely of Rocky composition, they are thus mostly uninhabitable due to their lack of solid surface.

Earth's orbital neighborhood

Only 1 planet in the entire TESS catalog has an orbital year longer than 300 days. Earth orbits in 365. The red box shows where Earth-analog worlds should live. It is almost completely empty.

This is not because these planets don't exist. TESS observes each part of the sky for only ~27 days at a time — not long enough to detect a planet with a year-long orbit.

The telescope's blind spot

This isn't because Earth-like planets don't exist — it's because TESS cannot find them. Every catalog reflects the instrument that built it. The data below lets you explore the full picture.

Detection bias lens

The same planets, now mapped by how long their year is (X) versus how big they are (Y). This view reveals why the catalog looks the way it does.

X axis — Orbital period (days) How long the planet takes to orbit its star. Earth's year is 365 days. Most TESS planets orbit in under 13 days — closer to their star than Mercury is to the Sun. The log scale stretches the short-period end to make them visible.
Orange dashed line — TESS 27-day limit TESS surveys each strip of sky for approximately 27 days before moving on. To confirm a planet, astronomers need to observe at least two transits. Any planet with a period > 13 days is already hard to catch; beyond 27 days, detection becomes rare.
Red box — Earth-analog zone The region where a rocky Earth-like planet should live: period 300–430 days, radius 0.8–1.25 R⊕. Currently empty. This is not because these planets don't exist — it's because TESS is structurally unable to find them.
Dot color — Discovery year Lighter blue = earlier discovery (2018–2019), darker blue = recent (2025–2026). Use the timeline slider to animate how the catalog grew year by year and watch where new planets appear.

Transit probability: For TESS to detect Earth, our planet would need to pass directly in front of the Sun as seen from the spacecraft. The probability of any given star being aligned this way is R★/a ≈ 0.5% — only 1 in 200 star systems.

Transit signal depth: Earth's disk covers only 0.0084% of the Sun's disk — a brightness dip of ~84 parts per million. Most TESS targets are too faint and noisy to detect a signal this small. The Kepler mission, purpose-built for this, needed 4 years of continuous staring to confirm Earth-like planets.

Orbital period: At 365 days, Earth completes only one transit per year. TESS observes each sky sector for ~27 days. Catching a single transit is not enough to confirm a planet — you need multiple transits. Earth would require 13+ consecutive sectors (over a year) of uninterrupted coverage on the same star.

Verdict: TESS would almost certainly not detect Earth — even if the Sun were one of its target stars.

Where are they in the sky?

Every confirmed TESS planet plotted by its actual position on the celestial sphere. Color shows how hot the planet is. Size shows how close its star is to us.

Projection — Mollweide The full sky unrolled onto a flat ellipse. Right Ascension (RA) runs horizontally like longitude — measured in hours from 0h to 24h. Declination (Dec) runs vertically like latitude — from +90° (north pole) to −90° (south pole). The Milky Way runs diagonally across the map.
Dot color — Equilibrium temperature Cool blue dots are temperate worlds (below ~500 K). Warm pink/white dots are scorching hot planets (above 2,000 K). Most TESS planets are hot — a direct consequence of finding planets that orbit close to their stars.
Dot size — Distance to Earth Larger dots are nearby stars (under ~15 parsecs, or 50 light-years). Smaller dots are far away. Nearby planets are scientifically precious — they are the best candidates for follow-up atmospheric studies with JWST, the James Webb Space Telescope.
Clusters and patterns You may notice that planets are not uniformly spread across the sky. TESS observes in long rectangular "sectors" oriented to avoid the ecliptic plane. The coverage pattern reflects these sectors — some regions near the ecliptic poles were observed for much longer, producing denser clusters of discoveries.

A note on distance. The closest planetary system in this dataset is about 7 parsecs away — roughly 23 light-years. Light from those planets left before many of us were born. The farthest confirmed TESS planet is nearly 1,000 parsecs (3,260 light-years) away. Every dot on this map is a sun seen across centuries or millennia of travel time.

Discovery timeline

How the TESS exoplanet catalog grew year by year, colored by planet type. Click any colored band to filter all views to that planet class.

Why the ramp-up? TESS launched in April 2018 and began science operations mid-year. The first planets published in 2019 were low-hanging fruit: large planets on short orbits that were easy to confirm. As the mission matured and ground-based follow-up pipelines improved, confirmation rates accelerated sharply — 178 planets in 2024 alone.
Are smaller planets catching up? Watch the rocky (orange) and super-Earth (green) bands over time. Early years are dominated by gas giants. From 2022 onward, smaller planets make up a growing fraction — reflecting improved radial-velocity spectrographs that can measure the tiny stellar wobbles needed to confirm low-mass worlds.
Sub-Neptunes persist The blue sub-Neptune band is consistently the largest every year. This is partly physical (they are genuinely common) and partly instrumental (their size makes transits easy to detect and their mass makes RV confirmation feasible). They are TESS's bread and butter.

Build your own planet

Pick a star, tune the planet body and orbit, and watch the equilibrium temperature, insolation, period and Earth-similarity update in real time. Designed to make the physics of habitability tangible — most "obviously Earth-like" parameter sets fall apart the moment you change the host star.

Equilibrium temperature Derived from the Stefan–Boltzmann balance assuming a 30 % Bond albedo: Teq = T★ · √(R★/2a) · (1−A)¼. Surface temperature is typically higher once a real atmosphere is added.
Insolation flux S = L★/a² in Earth units. A planet with S = 2 receives twice the energy Earth does. The habitable zone roughly corresponds to S between 0.35 and 1.1 for Sun-like stars.
Habitable zone bounds Inner edge ≈ 0.95 · √L (Kopparapu et al. 2013, recent-Venus limit), outer edge ≈ 1.67 · √L (early-Mars limit). HZ width scales with the square root of stellar luminosity — bright stars push the zone outward, cool dwarfs squeeze it inward.
Surface gravity (g⊕) Computed as M/R² in Earth units. A planet with 1 g⊕ has the same gravity as Earth. Very high gravity makes liftoff (and biological mobility) hard; very low gravity makes it hard to retain an atmosphere.

Key concepts

Terms used throughout this visualization, explained for a general audience.

Equilibrium temperature
The temperature a planet's surface would reach if it absorbed all incoming starlight and radiated it back out evenly, with no atmosphere. Earth's is ~255 K (−18 °C). The greenhouse effect raises the actual surface temperature to ~288 K (+15 °C). Many exoplanets have equilibrium temperatures exceeding 1,000 K — hotter than molten lava.
Earth radius (R⊕)
A unit of planet size, where 1 R⊕ = 6,371 km (Earth's radius). Planets smaller than ~1.25 R⊕ are likely rocky like Earth or Mars. Between 1.25–4 R⊕ they are likely gaseous sub-Neptunes. Above 4 R⊕ they are Neptune-like or gas giant worlds.
Transit method
TESS finds planets by watching stars for tiny periodic dips in brightness — the shadow cast when a planet passes in front of the star. A planet the size of Earth blocks only 0.0084% of the Sun's light. Jupiter-sized planets block up to 1%. Bigger planets are easier to detect; this biases the catalog.
Habitable zone (HZ)
The orbital distance range where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface — often called the "Goldilocks zone." Its exact boundaries depend on the star's temperature and luminosity. For a Sun-like G star, the HZ spans roughly 0.95–1.37 au. For a cool red dwarf (M star), it shrinks inward to 0.1–0.4 au.
Fulton radius gap
A real scarcity of planets with radii between ~1.5 and ~2.0 R⊕, discovered in 2017 using Kepler data. The leading explanation: planets in this size range receive enough stellar radiation to completely strip their hydrogen/helium atmospheres (photoevaporation), leaving either bare rocky cores below the gap or failed giants with thick atmospheres above it.
Parsec (pc)
A unit of distance used in astronomy. 1 parsec ≈ 3.26 light-years ≈ 31 trillion km. The closest star to Earth (Proxima Centauri) is 1.3 pc away. The TESS exoplanets in this dataset range from ~7 pc to ~940 pc — meaning their light traveled between 23 and 3,000 years to reach us.
Detection bias
Any systematic distortion in a dataset caused by the way observations are made. TESS has strong detection biases toward: large planets (bigger transit signal), short orbital periods (more transits per observation window), and bright nearby stars (better signal-to-noise). This means the catalog is not a fair sample of what actually exists — it is a sample of what TESS can see.
Insolation flux (S⊕)
The amount of stellar energy hitting a planet's surface, measured relative to Earth (1 S⊕). A planet receiving 2 S⊕ gets twice the energy Earth does. This is a more physically complete measure of habitability than equilibrium temperature alone because it accounts for the actual stellar luminosity rather than assuming blackbody radiation.
